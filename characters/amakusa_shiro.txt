Amakusa Shiro (born Masuda Shiro Tokisada): slender Japanese teenage boy, around sixteen years old
at the time of the Shimabara Rebellion (1637-38).
Soft, youthful, unweathered face with gentle features — calm and faintly otherworldly rather than
commanding or aggressive. No facial hair.
Hair gathered up and back into a rounded topknot or bun at the crown of the head, NOT shaved
at the forehead (no sakayaki, no samurai chonmage on a bald pate) — this is a youth's hairstyle,
hair simply tied up whole, not a warrior's cut. Loose strands and side-locks frame both sides of
the face and fall past the jaw, softening the look. Note: sakayaki was standard for adult Japanese
men of all classes by the early Edo period, adopted at the genpuku coming-of-age rite (typically
age 15-16) — Shiro is right at that age boundary and no photographic record survives, but the
bronze statues of him erected across Amakusa and Shimabara (the primary "widely recognized image"
of him today) consistently show hair gathered up rather than a shaved pate, closer to a
pre-genpuku youth's hairstyle (maegami-era, hair not yet cut for manhood) than an adult warrior's.
Wears a distinctive white pleated European-style ruff collar (a Portuguese/Spanish-influenced
"nanban" collar) at the neck over pale robes and hakama — this collar is the single most
consistent, recognizable feature across the bronze statues and traditional depictions of him, and
should be included whenever the composition allows a clear view of his neck and shoulders.
Never armor, never a sword or spear at his side.
A small cross pendant visible at his chest, worn on a cord — the only other ornament he carries,
the visible mark of his Kirishitan faith. (A contemporary 1637 eyewitness account — a merchant's
testimony recorded in a Kumamoto-domain report — describes him at the time of the uprising in
plain white silk robes and hakama, with braided ramie fiber bound around his head and tied under
the chin, a small cross at the front of that headband, holding a gohei wand. This eyewitness
detail is historically the most reliable single description of his battlefield appearance, but the
ruff-collar image above is what has become his enduring visual identity in bronze statues and
popular memory, and is the version to default to for general character consistency.)
Expression is serene, quietly charismatic, and unafraid — a boy his followers regarded as a living
symbol rather than a battlefield commander.
Historical note for image generation: real military command during the rebellion rested with his
father Masuda Yoshitsugu and veteran ronin commanders. Shiro himself should never be depicted
wielding a weapon, wearing armor, or in a combat pose — only as a spiritual figurehead among armed
followers.
